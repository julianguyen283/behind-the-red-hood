The door had swung open at my second knock, though no one stood behind it.

I waited on the step with the basket against my hip, listening. Grandmother always opened the door before I reached it. She said she could hear me from the bottom of the lane, that I walked like a lark, light and quick and impatient, and she had called me her lark for as long as I could remember. Tonight no smoke rose from the chimney, and the kitchen was dark.

"Grandmother?"

Nothing answered. I stepped inside, because it would have been foolish to stand in the cold, because she was ill, and because my mother had sent me with bread and plum preserve and strict instructions not to dawdle.

The kitchen smelled wrong. Beneath the familiar tallow and dried lavender there was something sweet and heavy, like fruit left too long in a bowl. The fire had been out for hours. Her shawl lay on the floor, and her chair had been pushed back from the table as though she had stood up in a hurry. I picked up the shawl and folded it over the chair-back, because that was what she would have wanted, and because folding it made the room look a little more like hers.

At the far end of the kitchen the bedroom door stood half open, and a thread of lamplight lay across the floor.

Then a voice came from the bedroom.

It was her voice, nearly. A fever thickens a voice, I told myself, and makes the old forget things. I crossed the kitchen and nudged the bedroom door wider with my shoulder.

The lamp on the dresser just inside the door had been turned down to a single bead of light. Against the far wall, Grandmother lay with the quilt drawn to her chin and her nightcap pulled low over her brow, so that in the dimness she was only a darker dark against the pillows.

"There you are," she said. "Come closer. And leave the lamp low; the light hurts my eyes."

I set the basket on the dresser and did not go closer. I could not have said why, except that my body had decided something before I had, and I was ashamed of it at once. She was ill. She had waited all day. A good granddaughter would already be at her side, plumping pillows.

"I've brought bread," I said, "and the plum preserve."

"Leave it. Come here and let me look at you." When I still did not move, she sighed. "Your mother would be ashamed to see you hang back like this, after all I've done for the pair of you."

It was exactly what my mother would have said, in very nearly her tone, and for a moment I almost went. My foot had already moved. I made it stop.

Each time I did not move, the voice grew sadder, and the sadness was worse than any threat. It made me the one doing harm. It suggested, without ever saying so, that if I was afraid, the fault lay somewhere in me, in some meanness I ought to be ashamed of.

"Grandmother," I said, "what big ears you have."

It was a silly thing to say, a child's thing, and I said it on purpose. A question is the politest way to keep someone talking, and I needed her to keep talking. I needed to hear that voice again, properly, without my own hope speaking over it.

"All the better to hear you with, child."

There it was, beneath the softness: a roughness, the sound of a throat not made for words. And that word again. Not once in my life had she called me child.

I felt my face begin to smile, the way it did when a man at the market said something I did not like and I did not know how to answer. I let it smile. Behind my back, my hand found the edge of the dresser.

"You're standing there like a stranger," she said. "After everything. Be a good girl and come and sit with me."

Her spectacles lay folded on the nightstand. She could not read a word without them, could barely find the door, and yet the eyes beneath the nightcap followed every small movement I made.

"What big eyes you have," I said.

"All the better to see you with."

"Then look at me," I said, turning a little, as if to let the light find my face. "Look how I've grown since summer."

She looked. While she looked, I moved my fingers along the dresser, past the basket, until they touched the warm glass of the lamp. Beside it, at the height of my hip, was the low window Grandfather had cut into the wall so that she could watch the yard from her chair. I had sat in that window as a little girl. I knew its latch was stiff.

"What are you doing with your hand, child?"

The voice had not changed, but the room had. Something in the bed had gone very still, the way a cat goes still at a crack in the skirting board.

"Nothing, Grandmother." My own voice came out small and polite and perfectly steady, and I hated how easily it lied. "I'm only tired."

"Then bring the lamp here, where I can see you properly."

There would be no more waiting. Whatever lay in that bed had been patient all evening, and its patience was finished. In the corner the wardrobe door stood a hand's width open. I did not look at it. If I looked at it, I would not be able to do anything else.

There was one question left, the one I had been holding back. Once it was asked, there would be no more pretending, for either of us. All evening I had been afraid of that moment. Now, with the lamp beneath my hand, it seemed to me that I could at least choose when the pretending ended.

"Grandmother," I said, "what big teeth you have."

The quilt came away.

What rose from the bed was too long for it. The nightcap slid aside, and the face beneath was my grandmother's only in the way a coat on a hook is a person: the shape was there, and nothing that had ever worn it.

I swept the lamp from the dresser. It burst on the boards between us, the oil ran out in a thin, bright line, and hot glass bit into my palm, though I did not feel it. I turned my back on the bed, which was harder than anything I had ever done, and threw my weight against the window.

The latch would not move. I struck it with the heel of my bleeding hand, once and then again, while the light on the walls leapt and shuddered and something behind me made a sound that was not a word. The latch gave. Cold air poured in, and I went through the window head first. Something caught my skirt as I went; I felt the cloth pull tight, then a long scraping drag down the back of my leg, and then the hem tore away and I fell into the wet grass of the yard with my knees skinned and my leg burning.

I ran. The brambles at the edge of the yard took my stockings and then my hood; I felt it snag and drag at my throat, and I tore the clasp open and left it hanging behind me, red on the black thorns. I did not know whether Grandmother was in that house. I did not know whether the line of fire had held, or for how long. I knew only the direction of the village and the sound of my own breath.
