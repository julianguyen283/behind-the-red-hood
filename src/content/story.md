The door swung open at my second knock, and no one stood behind it.

I stayed on the step with the basket against my hip, waiting for her voice. Grandmother always heard me before I reached the gate. By the time I knocked she was usually there already, wiping her hands on her apron and calling me her lark. Tonight the hinges finished their long complaint, and the house said nothing at all.

"Grandmother?"

No smoke rose from the chimney. I went in, because she was ill, because it was cold, and because my mother had sent me with bread and plum preserve and told me not to dawdle.

[[scene:kitchen]]

In the grey light from the window, the kitchen looked as though she had stepped out of it a moment ago and never come back. The fire had been dead for hours. Her shawl lay on the floor beside her chair, and her knitting waited on the table, the needles still through a half-finished row. Beneath the old smells of tallow and lavender there was something sweet and heavy, like fruit left too long in a bowl. I picked up the shawl and folded it over the chair-back, because she hated things left on the floor, and because folding it made the kitchen look a little more like hers.

At the far end of the room the bedroom door stood half open, and a thread of lamplight lay across the boards.

Then she spoke to me from the bedroom.

[[audio:audio-1]]

It was her voice, nearly. And it had not called me lark. Illness thickens a voice, I told myself, and a fever makes the old forget the names they have used for years. I crossed the kitchen, nudged the bedroom door wider with my shoulder, and stopped on the threshold.

[[scene:bedroom1]]

[[light:lamp]]

The room was small and close. Against the far wall, Grandmother lay with the quilt drawn up to her chin and her nightcap pulled down, so that she was only a darker dark against the pillows. To my left, an arm's length inside the door, the lamp stood on the dresser, turned down to a single bead of flame. I stepped in, and behind me the door drifted shut on the sloping floor, as it always had.

"There you are," she said. "Come here. And leave that lamp as it is; the light hurts my eyes."

I set the basket on the dresser and did not go to her. I could not have said why, and I was ashamed of it at once. She was ill. She had been alone all day. Last winter, when I came in with my fingers white from the cold, she had taken my hands between hers and held them against her neck until I could feel them again.

"Your hands will be frozen," she said, as if she had heard me remembering. "Bring them here."

I very nearly went. My foot had moved before I made it stop.

"Your mother would be hurt to see you hang back like this," she said, more softly. "After everything."

[[scene:bedroom2]]

[[light:head]]

"Grandmother," I said, "what big ears you have."

It had been our game when I was small: ears, then eyes, then teeth. She had her own answer for each, and the first was always "All the better to hear you sing, my lark," and then she would make me sing until I laughed too much to finish.

[[ghost:"All the better to hear you sing, my lark."]]

"All the better to hear you with, child."

Not her answer. Not my name. Under the softness was a rasp, as though the throat had been made for other sounds and was only borrowing these.

My face began to smile, the way it did when a man at market said something I did not like and I could think of nothing to say. I let it. Behind me the door was shut, and beyond it lay the whole length of the kitchen. I had seen how fast a dog can cross a yard. I would not get halfway. My fingers found the edge of the dresser.

"You're standing there like a stranger," she said. "Be a good girl and come and sit with me."

[[room:pause]]

[[light:bedpost]]

Little by little the room came out of the gloom. On the bedpost at the foot of the bed, the wood was scored with fresh, pale scratches, four together, that had not been there in summer. Her spectacles lay folded on the nightstand; without them she could barely find her own slippers, and yet the eyes beneath the nightcap followed every small movement I made. I wanted those eyes on my face and nowhere else.

[[scene:bedroom3]]

[[light:head]]

"What big eyes you have," I said.

"All the better to see you with."

"Then look at me." I turned a little, so the light would find my face. "Look how I've grown since summer."

She looked. "You've grown sharp," she said at last. "Your mother never mentioned that."

[[light:lamp]]

While she was still looking, I slid my hand along the dresser, past the basket, until it touched the lamp's warm brass base. Just beyond the dresser, in the same wall, at the height of my hip, was the window Grandfather had cut so that she could see the gate from her bed. I had sat in it as a child, and I knew its latch stuck when the wood swelled in wet weather.

"What are you doing with your hand, child?"

The voice had not changed, but the room had. The shape under the quilt had gone very still.

"Nothing, Grandmother." My voice came out small and polite and perfectly steady, and I hated how easily it lied. "I'm tired, that's all."

"Then bring the lamp here, where I can see you properly."

[[light:wardrobe]]

So it knew. Perhaps it had known since I stopped on the threshold, and had only been waiting to see what I would do. In the corner, the wardrobe door stood a hand's width open. I did not look at it. If I looked at it, I would not be able to do anything else.

I closed my fingers around the base of the lamp. There was one question left in the game. It had answered the other two the way the old rhyme goes, quickly and with pleasure, as if it had been waiting all evening to say them, and it would want to say this one most of all.

[[scene:silence]]

[[light:out]]

"Grandmother," I said, "what big teeth you have."

"All the better—" it began, and the quilt came away.

[[sfx:rise]]

[[light:jolt]]

What rose from the bed was too long for it. The nightcap slid aside, and the face beneath was my grandmother's only in the way a coat on a hook is a person: the shape was there, and no one inside it.

[[sfx:glass]]

[[light:fire]]

I threw the lamp. It burst on the boards between us, and the spilled oil caught and ran in a thin line of flame, blue at the root. The thing from the bed came at it and flinched back, then came again along the wall, where the flame was thinnest. I turned my back on it and threw my weight against the window.

[[sfx:latch]]

The latch would not lift. I struck it with the heel of my hand, once, twice, until the skin split, while the light jumped on the walls and the thing beyond the fire made a sound that was not a word. On the third blow the swollen wood gave. Cold air poured in, and I went through head first. Halfway out, something caught my skirt. The cloth drew tight, a long, tearing pain went down the back of my leg, and then the hem ripped free and I fell into the wet grass of the yard.

[[scene:night]]

[[sfx:run]]

My leg would not take my weight properly. I ran anyway, half stumbling. At the edge of the yard the brambles caught my hood and pulled it tight against my throat; I tore the clasp open and left it hanging behind me, red on the black thorns.[[thread-end]] I did not know whether Grandmother was in that house, behind the wardrobe door I had not let myself look at. I did not know whether the fire had held, or for how long. I knew only the way to the village and the sound of my own breath.
